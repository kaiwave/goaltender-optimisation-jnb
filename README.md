# A Projective Geometry Analysis for Optimal Goaltender Positioning in Hockey

A 3D projective geometry model of ice hockey goaltender positioning. It quantifies the trade-off behind "crease aggression": a goaltender who challenges forward cuts down the exposed net area of direct shots, but is slower to slide across the crease against passes.

The report, the analysis and the figures all live in a single Jupyter notebook (`main.ipynb`), backed by a plain Python module (`model.py`). Figures are drawn with matplotlib.

> **Status:** Complete. The entire report has been written up and published on [cloud.kaiwave.dev](https://cloud.kaiwave.dev/s/soEgMwqb2sbCaD2) - with a card on [kaiwave.dev/projects](https://kaiwave.dev/projects/). 

## The idea

- **Static case.** The goaltender is a rectangular plane ($1.15m \times 0.95m$) held square to the puck's line of sight at depth $d_g$ from the goal line midpoint. Its four corners are perspective-projected from the puck onto the goal plane, and the exposed net area $A_{eff}$ is the net rectangle minus the projected silhouette. Shot release is drawn from a normal distribution around a nominal play spot, so what matters is the *expected* exposed area $E[A_{eff}]$.

- **Dynamic case.** For a pass $\vec{p}_1 \to \vec{p}_2$, the puck's transit time is compared with the goaltender's recovery time (reaction time plus a lateral slide along the crease). The optimal dynamic depth $d_g^*$ is the deepest position at which the probability of arriving late, $P_{late}$, stays at or below $\alpha = 0.05$.

## Model parameters

All are constants at the top of `model.py`. Distances are in metres and times in seconds.

| Parameter | Value | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `W_NET`, `H_NET` | 1.83, 1.22 | NHL net width and height |
| `R_CREASE` | 1.83 | Crease radius, and the upper bound on $d_g$ |
| `W_GOALIE`, `H_GOALIE` | 1.15, 0.95 | Idealised butterfly silhouette |
| `V_PASS` | 25 m/s | Constant puck pass speed |
| `V_SLIDE` | 3.5 m/s | Constant lateral butterfly slide speed |
| `T_REACT` | 0.2 s | Visual-motor reaction time |
| `SIGMA_X_DEFAULT`, `SIGMA_Y_DEFAULT` | 0.40, 0.30 | Release spread (standard deviations) in x and y |
| `N_SAMPLES` | 1000 | Monte Carlo samples per evaluation |
| `RISK_THRESHOLD` | 0.05 | Acceptable late-recovery probability $\alpha$ |

Coordinates: origin at the midpoint of the goal line, $x$ across the rink, $y$ out into the offensive zone, $z$ up from the ice.

## Main findings

- Forward depth is only strongly protective against close, central threats. Its benefit shrinks with shooting distance, and at wide angles the exposure gradient steepens and release uncertainty is penalised most heavily.

- Passing threats limit the safe depth well below the static ideal. At $\alpha = 0.05$, the reports finds $d_g^* \approx$ $0.55 m$ for the seam pass, $0.01 m$ for the goal line feed and $0.90 m$ for the breakaway one-timer.

- Optimal crease depth is a joint function of shot angle, release uncertainty and the proximity and centrality of the next passing option, not a fixed "aggressive" or "conservative" style.

## Known limitations

- The goaltender is a rigid rectangle with no pad, stick or glove geometry, no post integration, and no screens or deflections. Pucks are released at ice level.

- Pass speed, slide speed and reaction time are constants, with no acceleration phase. The reaction time comes from a general study, not from goaltenders.

- The release covariance is assumed, not estimated from tracking data, and the model has not been validated against observed save percentages.

- The bounded static solver `solve_dg_static` returns the upper bound $d_g = 1.83$ m for all four static scenarios under the default $\sigma$ values, so the static figures are drawn at that depth. The static and dynamic problems are optimised separately, not jointly.
